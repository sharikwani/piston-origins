Nissan GT-R R35 vs Porsche 911 Turbo: Specs, Prices, Laps

Updated 2026-11-09 · 10 sources

GT-R vs 911 Turbo: why they get compared

For most of the 2000s, the Porsche 911 Turbo was the default answer for anyone wanting a supercar they could use every day: four-wheel drive, two small rear seats and enough performance to embarrass far more exotic machinery. When Nissan launched the R35 GT-R in late 2007, it aimed squarely at that car, with the same headline power, the same all-wheel-drive formula and a price close to half. The comparison has stuck ever since, through every generation of both cars.

This page compares the R35 with the 997-generation 911 Turbo it was launched against, then looks briefly at how the rivalry developed. The story of the 2008 Nürburgring row is told in full in our episode article.

GT-R R35 vs 997 Turbo specs

On paper, the two cars matched exactly on power. Nissan rated the GT-R's 3.8-litre twin-turbo VR38DETT V6 at 480 PS at 6,400 rpm, with 588 Nm from 3,200 to 5,200 rpm. Porsche's 997 Turbo, launched in June 2006, also produced 480 PS (353 kW), from a 3.6-litre twin-turbo flat-six. Its 620 Nm of torque arrived from just 1,950 rpm, and Porsche's newsroom says the optional Sport Chrono Package Turbo could raise it to 680 Nm for up to 10 seconds on overboost.

The layouts are mirror images. The 911 Turbo keeps its engine behind the rear axle, as every 911 has, with permanent all-wheel drive and a six-speed manual or Tiptronic S automatic. The GT-R puts its engine at the front and its six-speed dual-clutch gearbox at the rear as a transaxle, with ATTESA E-TS all-wheel drive. Jalopnik's reproduction of Nissan's June 2008 US release gives a drag coefficient of 0.27.

The biggest gap is weight. CarExpert lists the 2009 GT-R at 1,740 kg and the 2007 911 Turbo at 1,585 kg, so the Nissan carried around 155 kg more. That makes its performance all the more striking.

Prices: GT-R vs 911 Turbo at launch

Price was the GT-R's sharpest weapon. In the US, the 2009 GT-R cost $69,850 and the GT-R Premium $71,900, plus a $1,000 destination charge, according to Nissan's release. MotorWeek gave the base price of the 997 Turbo as $122,900, with its test car at $141,715 once options such as $8,500 ceramic brakes were added.

The UK picture was similar. PistonHeads records a GT-R launch price of £56,795 when it went on sale in late 2008, rising to £69,950 by October 2010. When evo ran a three-way test in February 2010, it put the GT-R at £58,000 and described it as "more or less half the price" of the top-spec 911 Turbo it was testing.

0–60 times and road tests

Tested acceleration favoured the Nissan. MotorWeek recorded 0 to 60 mph in 3.5 seconds for the GT-R using launch control. In its 997 Turbo test, it recorded 3.9 seconds and a 12.2-second quarter mile at 120 mph, and Porsche quoted 3.9 seconds to 100 km/h for the manual car.

Road testers were split less than the stopwatch suggests. Auto Express, in its used GT-R buying guide, says the 997 Turbo offers similar levels of performance, build quality and usability, with the added choice of a manual gearbox and a Cabriolet. evo's February 2010 test, which put the facelifted 911 Turbo against the GT-R and an Audi R8 V10 on Welsh roads, chose the Nissan, concluding that it had not found an all-weather supercar more appealing than the GT-R.

The 2008 Nürburgring lap time dispute

Nissan claimed a lap of the Nürburgring Nordschleife in 7:29 for the GT-R, quicker than Porsche's own 911 GT2. In 2008 Porsche bought a US-specification GT-R, tested it and recorded 7:54, against 7:38 for its own 911 Turbo, and suggested Nissan had used non-standard tyres. Nissan replied that the car had been on standard showroom tyres and pointed Porsche towards its driver training. Neither side ever conceded, and the episode article covers the full exchange. Nissan's later laps, culminating in 7:08.679 for the GT-R Nismo in 2013, did much of its arguing for it.

Running costs and ownership

The GT-R's lower purchase price did not make it a cheap car to run. Auto Express, writing in 2018, says cars up to and including the 2010 model year need servicing every six months or 6,000 miles, later cars every 12 months or 9,000 miles, and that a typical service costs £500 to £700 if brakes are not needed. It quotes £460 for the gearbox oil change due every two years or 18,000 miles, and notes that gear selection problems are common on pre-2011 cars.

We could not find an equally well-sourced, dated breakdown of 997 Turbo running costs, so we have not quoted one. Buyers should get a specialist inspection and a written service quote for either car.

Depreciation narrowed the price gap for used buyers. PistonHeads reported in November 2014 that early GT-Rs were widely available from around £35,000 for a 2009 car, and Auto Express put the earliest cars at about £35,000 in 2018.

Later generations: GT-R vs 911 Turbo after the 997

Both cars kept improving. Porsche's 2010 facelift brought a new 3.8-litre engine with 500 PS and 650 Nm, plus the seven-speed PDK gearbox. The 991 Turbo of 2013 made 520 PS and added active rear-wheel steering, and the 992 Turbo of 2020 made 580 PS, according to Porsche's newsroom.

The GT-R's power climbed in steps over its 18-year life, and by the end of production in 2025 the standard car made 570 PS and the GT-R Nismo 600 PS. It stayed on the same basic platform throughout, while Porsche replaced the 911 twice. The latest 911 Turbo S, with T-Hybrid assistance and 711 PS, lapped the Nordschleife in 7:03.92 during testing in autumn 2024, as confirmed by the Nürburgring in September 2025, nearly five seconds quicker than the GT-R Nismo's best.

The fair verdict on the original rivalry is that the GT-R matched or beat the 997 Turbo on performance for roughly half the money, while the Porsche offered a lighter car, a manual gearbox, an open-top option and a longer-established ownership network. Which was the better buy depended on whether the buyer valued performance per pound or the 911's breadth.
