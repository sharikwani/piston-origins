2JZ vs RB26: Which Twin-Turbo Inline-Six Is Better?

Updated 2026-11-09 · 10 sources

2JZ vs RB26: the short answer

The Toyota 2JZ-GTE and the Nissan RB26DETT are the two most argued-over engines of Japan's 1990s performance boom. Both are cast-iron, 24-valve, twin-turbocharged inline-sixes with belt-driven twin camshafts, both carried the same 280 PS (about 276 hp) official rating at home, and both now power builds making several times that. HP Academy says Toyota developed the 2JZ-GTE partly as an answer to the RB26, which had arrived in the R32 Skyline GT-R in 1989.

The honest verdict is that they were built for different jobs. The RB26 was shaped by racing rules and designed to rev; the 2JZ-GTE was a bigger, torquier road engine engineered with a large margin of strength. Which is "better" depends on what you want the engine to do, what it is going into and how much you are prepared to spend.

Design: a racing engine and a road engine

The RB26DETT's 2,568 cc capacity was not an accident. Japanese magazine Best Car says Nissan sized it to win Group A touring-car racing, where a 1.7 multiplier for turbocharged engines put a 2.6-litre car in the 4,500 cc class. Everything about it points towards high engine speed: a short 73.7 mm stroke, six individual throttle bodies, solid lifters with shims, and ceramic-wheeled turbochargers sized for quick response. In the R32 GT-R it recorded 29 wins and no defeats in the Japan Touring Car Championship from 1990 to 1993, according to Nissan.

The 2JZ-GTE took a different route. Its 2,997 cc comes from a square 86 mm bore and 86 mm stroke, so for the same bore it has a much longer stroke than the RB26 and more displacement to work with. It uses a single throttle body and two turbochargers working in sequence, one at low revs and both at high revs, which favours a broad spread of torque on the road. The deeper story of each engine sits in our separate 2JZ-GTE and RB26DETT guides.

Block, crank and head compared

Both blocks are cast iron with aluminium heads, but the details differ. The 2JZ-GTE has a fully closed-deck block, a forged steel crankshaft with 12 counterweights running in seven main bearings, oil squirters under the pistons and a girdle that stiffens the main bearing area. Jalopnik credits that combination for the engine's durability under heavy modification, and HP Academy says the main bearing caps, not the block itself, are usually the first part to give up on a highly tuned standard motor.

The RB26DETT also has oil squirters, sodium-filled exhaust valves and an eight-counterweight crank, but its reputation is more mixed. Wikipedia notes that the standard crank vibrates between 7,000 and 8,000 rpm, and early R32 engines have a narrow oil-pump drive that can break at high revs. MotoIQ's Mike Kojima is among the engine's harshest critics: in his list of performance engines he dislikes, he describes a "long whippy crank and a flexy block" and a "pathetically weak oil pump", and says even a well-built RB26 was "maintenance intensive". That is one engineer's opinion, but it reflects why serious RB26 builds usually budget for an upgraded oil pump, oil control and, in big builds, deck plates.

The RB26's advantage is at the top end. Its short stroke and solid-lifter valvetrain suit high engine speed, while the 2JZ-GTE's bucket-style lifters limit how aggressive its camshafts can be, according to a teardown reported by Motorious.

Turbochargers and factory output

On paper the two engines were equal in Japan, both rated at 280 PS under the manufacturers' gentlemen's agreement. Neither figure is the whole story. Wikipedia notes that enthusiasts generally believe the RB26 made nearer 320 PS, and Toyota itself rated the export 2JZ-GTE at 320 hp and 315 lb-ft for the US Supra Turbo. Torque is where displacement shows: Japanese ratings were 434 Nm, rising to 451 Nm with VVT-i, for the 2JZ-GTE, against 353 Nm for the R32 RB26 and 392 Nm for the late R34 engine.

Both engines' factory turbochargers are a limit once boost goes up. Japanese-market 2JZ-GTE turbos and all standard RB26 turbos use ceramic turbine wheels, which do not tolerate extra boost well. DSport says the RB26's factory turbos run out at about 400 hp at the wheels, while most big 2JZ-GTE builds replace the sequential pair with a single large turbocharger, according to Jalopnik.

2JZ vs RB26 strength: how tuners rate them

Most power claims for both engines come from tuners and magazines, not controlled tests, so they are best read as rules of thumb. For the 2JZ-GTE, Jalopnik reports that most tuners consider the standard bottom end good for around 800 hp. HP Academy is more cautious: it treats about 600 hp as a sensible limit for an engine driven hard on standard internals, recommends aftermarket main caps from around 800 hp and calls a standard engine run at 1,000 hp a "dyno queen" with a much shortened life.

For the RB26DETT, DSport says the standard block is good for more than 800 hp at the wheels and that fully built engines have passed 1,400 hp. Kojima's view, quoted above, is that the block starts to "flex and beat things up" as power rises into the 500s. The two positions are not as far apart as they sound: both camps agree that an RB26 needs oiling upgrades and more care than a 2JZ-GTE to live at high power. The fair summary is that the 2JZ-GTE has the larger safety margin on standard parts, while the RB26 can be built to similar outputs with more investment.

Parts supply and running costs

Both makers now support their old engines, which matters more each year. HP Academy reports that Toyota still sells new VVT-i 2JZ-GTE blocks and cylinder heads, though not the earlier non-VVT-i versions, and Toyota's GR Heritage Parts programme has been remaking A80 Supra parts since 2020.

Nissan went further for the RB26. Nismo's Heritage Parts programme began in 2017 with R32 parts, and in March 2019 Jalopnik and Japanese Nostalgic Car reported that it had added new RB26DETT engine blocks at ¥170,000 for the R32, R33 and R34, an N1-type block at ¥425,000 for the R33 and R34, cylinder heads, fuel pumps and engine harnesses. Prices quoted then may have changed since.

The aftermarket for both engines is huge. On cost, DSport lists the price of upgrades among the RB26's drawbacks, alongside its smaller displacement, and the oiling work its critics recommend adds to the bill before any extra power is made.

Which suits which build?

For a high-power street car, a drag car or an engine swap where torque and a margin of strength come first, the 2JZ-GTE is the easier choice. It has more displacement and torque, and tuners rate it to tolerate big power on standard internals with fewer upgrades.

For a car that will rev, a circuit build or a period-correct Skyline GT-R, the RB26DETT makes more sense. It was designed to rev hard and win races, and in a GT-R it is part of what makes the car what it is. It needs more attention to oiling and more money to reach big numbers, but Nissan's own new-block supply and a deep tuning culture mean it is far from a dead end. Neither engine is the wrong answer; they suit different builds.
