Lexus LFA History: The V10 Supercar Toyota Lost Money On

Video: Toyota Lost Money on Every Lexus LFA. Owners Didn't.

19 October 2026 · 4:22 · 131 views on YouTube

In 2010, Toyota sold the Lexus LFA for $375,000 and still lost money on every single one. Today one has sold for $1.81 million. Here's the full story of Toyota's craziest supercar.

The 4.8-litre V10 built with Yamaha revved from idle to 9,000 rpm in 0.6 seconds, so fast that a needle couldn't keep up and Lexus fitted a digital rev counter. Toyota threw away five years of work on an aluminium car and started again in carbon fibre, tuned the engine's sound like a musical instrument, chased the Nürburgring, and built only 500 cars by hand.

A $375,000 supercar that never paid for itself

In 2010, Toyota put a price of $375,000 on a car and, by the accounts of Car and Driver, still lost money on every single one it sold. That car was the Lexus LFA. It took around ten years to develop, only 500 were made, and in April 2026 one sold on Bring a Trailer for $1.81 million.

It is a strange story for a company known for being careful. Toyota built its fortune on sensible, profitable cars, yet here it was funding a hand-built V10 supercar that critics said cost too much and analysts said would never make a profit. To understand why, you have to go back to where the idea started.

How the Lexus LFA started: a bar in Hokkaido

The LFA story begins in 2000, in a bar in Hokkaido. A Toyota engineer named Haruhiko Tanahashi told his boss he wanted to build "the ultimate sports car". His boss did not laugh. He asked, "Why not?"

At the time Toyota was selling Camrys and Priuses, the kind of cars that pay the bills rather than set pulses racing. So the project began quietly, with a code name, TXS, and a tiny team. What happened next set the tone for the whole car: the first thing they perfected was not the body or the chassis, but the sound.

The Yamaha V10: 9,000 rpm and a digital rev counter

To build the engine, Toyota brought in Yamaha, a company that also makes pianos and motorcycles. Together they developed the 1LR-GUE, a 4.8-litre V10 that revved to 9,000 rpm and produced 552 hp.

The headline figure was not the power but the speed of the revs. The V10 could spin from idle to redline in 0.6 seconds. That was so quick that a conventional analogue needle physically could not keep up, so Lexus gave the LFA a digital tachometer instead. The engine had, quite literally, outrun the gauge.

Then the engineers tuned the engine like an instrument. The acoustic team studied a Formula One car at full revs, shaped the intake chamber after the body of a string instrument, and built three sound channels that carry the engine's voice directly into the cabin. Toyota's own engineers had a name for the result: "the roar of an angel".

From aluminium to carbon fibre: five years thrown away

By 2005 the car was close to finished, and then Toyota threw it away. The LFA had been engineered in aluminium, but Toyota's head of research, Kazuo Okamoto, had seen enough. His instruction, as reported, was blunt: "Get over it. Just go with carbon fiber."

Five years of work were scrapped. Toyota did not simply swap materials, either; it brought carbon fibre production in-house. The finished body was about 65% carbon fibre and roughly 100 kilos lighter than before. That saving came at a cost: the curing process limited the factory to about one car a day.

The Nürburgring, Hiromu Naruse and Akio Toyoda

From 2004, the LFA effectively lived at the Nürburgring, one of the most punishing circuits in the world. Its master test driver, Hiromu Naruse, shaped how the car drove, lap after lap on the Nordschleife.

The project reached the very top of the company. In 2009, Akio Toyoda, Toyota's future president, raced an LFA in the Nürburgring 24 Hours under an assumed name.

The development story also carries a tragedy. In June 2010, months before production began, Naruse was killed in a crash near the track while testing an LFA. He never saw the finished car, but the way every LFA drives owes a great deal to his work.

How many Lexus LFAs were made, and what they cost

Production ran from December 2010 to December 2012 at Toyota's Motomachi plant, where the cars were built by hand at a rate of about 20 a month. In total, 500 were made, each priced at $375,000.

The reaction was not kind. Critics said the LFA cost too much, analysts said it would never make a profit, and according to Car and Driver, Toyota lost money on every one. Then came the LFA Nürburgring Package, with more power, a fixed rear wing and a lap of the Nordschleife in 7 minutes 14.64 seconds in August 2011, one of the fastest times for a production car at that point.

Why Toyota built the LFA anyway

So why would the most careful car company on Earth build a car it knew would never pay for itself? Because the LFA was never really about profit. It taught Toyota how to work with carbon fibre, how to engineer sound, and how to build speed at the highest level. It also proved that Lexus could build a true supercar.

And there is a twist. Toyota may have lost money on every LFA, but the owners did not. A car that critics called overpriced at $375,000 has since changed hands for $1.81 million.

Quick answers

Did Toyota lose money on the Lexus LFA?

Yes. Toyota sold the Lexus LFA for $375,000 and still lost money on every one of the 500 cars it built by hand between 2010 and 2012. In April 2026 an LFA sold on Bring a Trailer for $1.81 million.

Why does the Lexus LFA have a digital rev counter?

Its 4.8-litre V10 could rev from idle to 9,000 rpm in 0.6 seconds, faster than a mechanical needle could follow, so Lexus fitted a digital rev counter.

Why did the Lexus LFA switch from aluminium to carbon fibre?

After about five years of development on an aluminium body, Toyota restarted the car in carbon-fibre-reinforced plastic to make it lighter and stiffer.
