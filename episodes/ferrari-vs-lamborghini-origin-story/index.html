Ferrari vs Lamborghini: How a Broken Clutch Started a Rivalry

Video: Ferrari vs Lamborghini: The Insult That Started It All

24 October 2026 · 7:44 · 322 views on YouTube

Ferruccio Lamborghini was a tractor maker who drove Ferraris. When the clutch kept failing, he went to Maranello to complain, and Enzo Ferrari supposedly told him he'd never handle a Ferrari. This is the full Lamborghini vs Ferrari origin story.

He built a V12 rival in four months, showed a car with no engine, then shocked the world with the Miura, the car that invented the supercar. Was the insult real, or a story Ferruccio liked to tell? And who actually won: Ferrari or Lamborghini?

Ferruccio Lamborghini, the tractor maker

Ferruccio Lamborghini was born in 1916 on a farm near Cento in northern Italy, and from the start he cared more about the machines than the fields. During the Second World War he served as an air force mechanic on the island of Rhodes, running the unit that kept the vehicles going.

When the war ended, Italy was full of abandoned military vehicles. In 1948 Ferruccio began turning them into tractors, and because farmers needed them, Lamborghini Trattori boomed. In 1959 he added a second business making oil burners, then moved into air conditioning, and he became very rich.

The Ferrari clutch problem

Rich Italians of the period bought Ferraris, and Ferruccio was no exception. He owned a Ferrari 250 GT coupé, then a 250 GT SWB Berlinetta, then a 250 GT 2+2. One thing kept going wrong on them: the clutch. Again and again his cars went back to Maranello to be rebuilt.

When Ferruccio looked inside, the story goes, he found that the clutch parts were essentially the same ones he was already using in his tractors. That was what made him angry enough to ask to see Enzo Ferrari himself.

Did Enzo Ferrari really insult Lamborghini?

The famous exchange comes from Ferruccio's own account, given in a 1991 interview. As he told it, he said to Enzo that his cars were rubbish, and Enzo replied that Lamborghini might be able to drive a tractor but would never be able to handle a Ferrari properly.

Not everyone close to the company has accepted that version. Lamborghini's long-serving test driver Valentino Balboni believes it. His predecessor, Bob Wallace, doubted it. The historian Massimo Delbo put it neatly: something was said, but only Enzo and Ferruccio knew what.

Ferruccio's biographer offers a more practical explanation. If tractor-grade parts were going into expensive sports cars, the profit on each one had to be enormous, and a businessman would notice that. Insult or business opportunity? Probably both.

Automobili Lamborghini and the 350 GTV with no engine

Automobili Lamborghini was founded on 30 October 1963, with a brand-new factory at Sant'Agata Bolognese, a short drive from Ferrari. To design the engine, Ferruccio hired Giotto Bizzarrini, who had left Ferrari two years earlier. Bizzarrini produced a V12 so extreme it was essentially a racing engine, which was not what Ferruccio wanted for a comfortable grand tourer. The two argued, and Ferruccio refused to pay his fee.

That same month the first Lamborghini, the 350 GTV, appeared at the Turin Motor Show. It had one problem: there was no engine in it. The finished production car, the 350 GT, arrived at Geneva in 1964, and 120 were built. Respectable, but hardly a Ferrari killer.

The Lamborghini Miura: the car that invented the supercar

The breakthrough came from three young men, all in their mid-twenties: engineer Gian Paolo Dallara, designer Marcello Gandini and test driver Bob Wallace. Their idea was to take the V12, turn it sideways and mount it behind the driver.

In 1965 Lamborghini showed only the bare chassis at Turin. Then, in March 1966 at Geneva, the complete Miura was revealed: low, wide and startlingly beautiful. It was the fastest production car in the world, capable of close to 280 km/h, and Lamborghini went on to build just over 750 of them. Today it is widely called the car that invented the supercar, and it came from a company whose founder made tractors.

Why Lamborghini uses a raging bull

The name has its own origin. In 1962 Ferruccio visited the ranch of Don Eduardo Miura near Seville, home of some of Spain's fiercest fighting bulls, and he loved them. He was also a Taurus. So when he started his car company, he put a raging bull on the badge, and the Miura was named after those bulls.

It began a tradition. Espada means a matador's sword, and Islero, Diablo and Murciélago are all names linked to fighting bulls. Ferrari had its prancing horse; Lamborghini had its bull.

How Ferrari responded to the mid-engined Miura

Enzo Ferrari, the man who reportedly said Ferruccio could not handle a Ferrari, believed a mid-engined road car would be too difficult for ordinary customers to drive. His own engineers needed years to convince him otherwise. Ferrari's first mid-engined 12-cylinder road car, the Berlinetta Boxer, did not arrive until 1973, seven years after the Miura.

Lamborghini kept moving. In 1968 came the Espada, a four-seat supercar, of which 1,227 were built. In 1971 the Countach prototype appeared at Geneva. Its name comes from a word in the Piedmontese dialect, a shout of amazement, and the story goes that it was exactly what Nuccio Bertone said when he first saw the car. Production began in 1974, and the Countach became the poster on a generation of bedroom walls.

How Ferruccio lost Lamborghini

While the cars grew wilder, Ferruccio's business grew harder. In 1972 he sold his tractor company and, in the same year, 51 per cent of the car company. In 1974 he sold the rest and walked away, retiring to a farm on Lake Trasimeno where he made wine.

Without him, Automobili Lamborghini went bankrupt in 1978. It was bought for $3 million in 1980, by Chrysler in 1987 for about $25 million, and by Audi in 1998 for about $110 million. Ferruccio Lamborghini died in 1993, five years before the Audi sale, so he never saw what his company became.

The numbers today show how close the rivalry has become. In 2025 Lamborghini delivered a record 10,747 cars, while Ferrari shipped 13,640. Ferrari is still bigger, but the company that, by its founder's account, began with a failing clutch and a sharp remark is now right behind it.

Quick answers

Why did Ferruccio Lamborghini start making cars?

Ferruccio Lamborghini was a tractor maker who owned Ferraris. When the clutch kept failing he complained to Enzo Ferrari, who, as Ferruccio told the story, said a tractor maker could never handle a Ferrari. Ferruccio decided to build his own grand tourer. His biographer argues the real motive was economic: there was money in high-end cars.

Was Lamborghini's first car really shown without an engine?

Yes. The Lamborghini 350 GTV was displayed at the 1963 Turin Motor Show without an engine. The production 350 GT followed at Geneva in 1964, and 120 were built.

Who sells more cars today, Ferrari or Lamborghini?

In 2025 Lamborghini delivered 10,747 cars and Ferrari shipped 13,640.
