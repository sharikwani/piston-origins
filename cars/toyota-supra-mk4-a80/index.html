Toyota Supra Mk4 (A80) Buyer's Guide: Specs, Values, Problems

Updated 2026-11-09 · 10 sources

Toyota Supra Mk4 (A80) at a glance

The fourth-generation Toyota Supra, chassis code JZA80, went on sale in Japan in May 1993 after a four-year development programme led by chief engineer Isao Tsuzuki, and stayed in Japanese production until 2002. The episode page tells the story of how it was designed; this page is a reference for owners and buyers. It covers what was sold where, how many were built, what goes wrong and what the cars are fetching now.

The A80 was a front-engined, rear-wheel-drive 2+2 coupé with double-wishbone suspension at both ends and a removable targa roof on many versions. Toyota sold it in Japan until 2002, but it left the UK at the end of 1996 and the US after the 1998 model year, which is why most cars on the market today are either early Western cars or later Japanese imports.

Toyota Supra Mk4 specs

Two 3.0-litre straight-sixes were offered. The naturally aspirated 2JZ-GE made 225 PS in Japanese trim, and the sequential twin-turbo 2JZ-GTE was rated at 280 PS (about 276 hp) in Japan under the domestic makers' gentlemen's agreement. Export ratings were higher: 320 hp for the US Supra Turbo and 326 bhp for the UK car, though evo's Adam Towler suggests the UK figure was optimistic and the real output nearer 300 bhp. Sources round the export figures slightly differently, so you will also see 321 hp and 330 PS quoted.

Turbo cars used Toyota's first six-speed manual, developed with Getrag, or a four-speed automatic; the non-turbo car had a five-speed manual or the same automatic. Kerb weights range from about 1,410 kg for an early non-turbo SZ to around 1,570 kg for a fully equipped turbo, and evo lists the UK car at 1,510 kg. European cars were limited to 155 mph, which Toyota's own UK history page calls "cruelly cut short".

Supra Mk4 trims, variants and special editions

Japan had the widest choice. The launch line-up was the non-turbo SZ, the turbo RZ (six-speed manual or automatic) and the turbo GZ, which was automatic only and carried more equipment. An SZ-R, a non-turbo car with sportier hardware, arrived in August 1994, and the automatic turbo RZ-S followed in May 1995. JDMBuySell's grade table, built from Toyota catalogues, notes that the SZ-R gained a six-speed manual in April 1996 and that ABS and twin front airbags became standard on every grade that month.

The US range was split between the naturally aspirated Supra and the Supra Turbo, while the UK received a single turbo model. In Japan both engines gained VVT-i variable valve timing in the late-1990s update; the US never received the VVT-i engine because Toyota stopped selling the car there after 1998.

The rarest factory-backed variant is the TRD 3000GT, a body-kitted car based on the JGTC racer. JDMBuySell, citing Toyota UK, counts 35 factory-built examples with numbered plates; many more cars wear replica or accessory kits, so a genuine one needs its plate and paperwork.

How many Mk4 Supras were made?

Toyota does not publish a single, easily found production total for the A80, so treat any round number with care. Sales figures are clearer for the export markets. Wikipedia's tabulation of North American sales comes to about 12,000 cars between 1993 and 1999, with the busiest years at launch. Toyota's UK history page says the A80 "notched up 600 sales" in Britain before UK sales stopped in 1996, and evo says "just over 600" were registered over three years.

How the A80 Supra was received

The A80 was quick from the start; the episode page covers its braking and acceleration figures. Its reputation as a driver's car took longer. evo's 2020 retrospective notes that launch-era testers found it "a bit aloof", a heavy grand tourer rather than an involving sports car, and that its later fame came from racing, the Fast and Furious films and Gran Turismo rather than from strong showroom sales. In the UK it cost £37,500 in 1993, rising to £41,327 by the end, which put it against established European rivals.

Motorsport and pop culture

Toyota's UK history page says the Mk4 won its class in Swiss mountain races, ran at Le Mans in two consecutive years, competed at Pikes Peak and in SCCA racing, and was a front-runner in the All-Japan GT Championship from 1995 to 2003. Off the track, the orange car from The Fast and the Furious made it famous well beyond tuning circles; that car sold for $550,000 at Barrett-Jackson Las Vegas in June 2021, still the benchmark price for any Supra.

What to look for when buying a Mk4 Supra

Modification is the biggest single issue. Many A80s have been tuned, and a car with a high-boost conversion, a single turbo or a manual swap should come with invoices showing who did the work. JDMBuySell, summarising a Road & Track buying guide, flags driveline whine under power, worn valve stem seals on older turbo engines (watch for oil smoke and consumption) and leaking targa roofs.

Classic & Sports Car's 2024 buyer's guide adds the basics that matter on any 30-year-old car: look thoroughly for accident damage, check the radiator and expansion tank for corrosion and leaks, check the coolant for signs of head-gasket failure, and watch for smoke on start-up, under load and on the overrun. It describes the gearboxes as robust but vulnerable to abuse, recommends fluid changes every 15,000 miles and suggests checking for jumping out of gear and clutch slip. It also warns that a missing catalytic converter on a UK car is likely to fail the MOT.

On Japanese imports, confirm the grade, engine and gearbox against the build year, because every A80 shares the JZA80 chassis code and catalogue specs changed over time. Parts support has improved: Toyota Gazoo Racing began remanufacturing selected A80 parts in 2020, as the 2JZ-GTE guide explains.

How much is a Mk4 Supra worth?

Values depend heavily on originality, mileage and whether the car is a manual turbo. At the top of the market, an unmodified 1994 Supra Turbo six-speed with 11,200 miles sold for $173,600 at RM Sotheby's Amelia Island in March 2019, against a 1994 sticker price of $42,800, according to The Drive. In June 2022 a 1998 Supra in Quicksilver with 22,962 miles sold for $265,000 hammer ($304,750 with premium) at a Paine Auctioneers sale, which The Drive described as a record for a non-movie car.

Ordinary cars change hands for far less. The Classic Valuer records a UK-registered 1995 Supra Turbo manual with 112,474 miles and a modified engine selling for £32,428 on PistonHeads in July 2026. JDMBuySell's listing data from 9 November 2026 puts the median asking price across 72 A80s at US$65,497, with the middle half between US$48,366 and US$77,324; those are asking prices, not sales.

Importing a Mk4 Supra into the US or UK

In the US, a car not built to federal standards can be imported under the 25-year exemption, counted from each car's own manufacture date. JDMBuySell notes that 2002 builds reach 25 years in 2027, and that the EPA generally expects the original engine configuration or an eligible certified replacement. State registration rules vary. US-market cars from 1993 to 1998 are already road legal, so many American buyers look at those first.

The UK sold A80s new, but most Supras on British roads are Japanese imports. Importing one means an import declaration, paying VAT and customs duty, telling HMRC within 14 days, meeting the vehicle approval rules, then registering and taxing the car with the DVLA; GOV.UK sets out these steps, and the details depend on the car's age and history, so check the current guidance before shipping.
