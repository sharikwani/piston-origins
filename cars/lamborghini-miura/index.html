Lamborghini Miura Buyer's Guide: P400, S and SV Specs, Values

Updated 2026-11-09 · 9 sources

What is the Lamborghini Miura?

The Miura is the mid-engined Lamborghini that is widely credited as the first supercar. Its engineers turned the company's 3.9-litre V12 sideways and placed it behind the two seats, and Bertone's Marcello Gandini, then 27, clothed it in one of the most admired shapes of the 1960s. Autocar India's 60th-anniversary history records that the bare rolling chassis appeared at the Turin show in November 1965, the finished car at Geneva in 1966, and production ran until 1973.

Our episode explains how a tractor maker came to build it. This page covers the car itself: the three production versions, how many were made and what an owner or buyer needs to know.

Lamborghini Miura specs

Every Miura uses the same basic layout: a 3.9-litre four-cam V12 mounted transversely behind the cabin, a five-speed manual gearbox and rear-wheel drive. Sotheby's notes that the engine was designed by Giotto Bizzarrini and that the chassis was conceived by Gian Paolo Dallara, Paolo Stanzani and Bob Wallace.

Published power figures differ by source, partly because they mix gross and net ratings and horsepower units. Autocar India gives 350hp for the P400, 370hp for the P400 S and 385hp for the SV. Classic Trader's buying guide lists 345bhp, 365bhp and 380bhp, and Sotheby's slightly lower again for the later cars. Lamborghini claimed a top speed of 280km/h and 0–100km/h in 6.7 seconds for the P400, which Autocar India says made it the fastest car in the world at the time. Classic Trader gives top speeds of 168 to 172mph across the three versions; RM Sotheby's quotes 180mph for the SV.

Miura P400, P400 S and P400 SV: the differences

The P400 (1966–1969) is the original. Sotheby's says it used thinner-gauge steel in its chassis than later cars, and Classic Trader notes that early cars' noses could lift at speeds above 170mph.

The P400 S (1968–1971) added power, a stiffer structure and more equipment. Sotheby's lists power windows, optional air conditioning, more luggage space, chrome trim and a revised interior, and Classic Trader says modifications were made to improve high-speed stability.

The P400 SV (1971–1973) is the final and most developed version. RM Sotheby's describes revised suspension to cure the front-end lightness, wider rear bodywork, larger carburettors and different cam timing. Sotheby's adds an optional limited-slip differential and the loss of the eyelash surrounds around the headlamps. The most important change for buyers came late in SV production: a split sump that separated engine and gearbox oil, fitted to what Classic Trader estimates were the last 96 or so cars.

How many Lamborghini Miuras were made?

The usual figures are 275 P400s, 338 P400 Ss and 150 SVs, a total of 763, as given by Autocar India and Classic Trader. Some auction catalogues put the SV at 142 or 148, and Goodwood's insurance guide speaks of 150 SVs plus one extra car, so treat the totals as close rather than exact.

Miura Jota, SVJ and the Roadster

Several one-offs and specials surround the production cars. The Jota was a competition-inspired car built by test driver Bob Wallace with about 440hp; Sotheby's says it crashed and burned in April 1971 and no longer exists. The SVJs that followed used Jota-derived parts; Sotheby's puts their number at six, with sources ranging from five to seven, and Goodwood says only four were originally made. One was built for the Shah of Iran. Goodwood also notes a single original Miura Roadster. Because conversions to SV or SVJ specification are common, Goodwood advises verifying any claimed special very carefully.

How the Miura was received

The Miura was the star of the 1966 Geneva show, and Autocar India describes it as one of the most striking cars ever made. It drew famous owners, including Frank Sinatra, Miles Davis and Eddie Van Halen, whose Miura S, Sotheby's says, provides the engine sound in the bridge of the 1984 song "Panama". Its best-known film role came in the opening sequence of The Italian Job (1969), where an orange Miura is driven through the Alps before being wrecked in a tunnel. Care of Carl reports that the film used a previously crashed car supplied by the factory for the crash scene, and that the driving car was rediscovered and certified in 2019.

Lamborghini Miura common problems

A Miura was not a reliable car when new, and the guides agree that condition varies enormously. WebCars, writing for owners, calls it fragile and temperamental. Every guide recommends an inspection by a Miura specialist and a documented history; Goodwood says certification by Lamborghini's heritage department, Polo Storico, makes a car a safer bet.

Rust is the first check. WebCars says most Miuras suffer from it because the original design drains poorly, and advises checking the frame for cracks. Goodwood names the front battery tray, engine frame and floor as rust areas and warns of crash damage, poor old repairs and misaligned frames.

On all but the late split-sump SVs, the engine and gearbox share oil. Classic Trader warns that without regular oil changes, metal from the gearbox can find its way into the engine, and WebCars advises shifting slowly and deliberately for the same reason. Goodwood says the gearbox needs warming up and a firm hand, and that most major mechanical work, including a clutch change, means removing the engine.

WebCars also highlights the fuel system: without proper venting, it says, Miuras can suffer engine-bay fires, and it recommends a pressurised fuel return line. The four Weber carburettors need specialist setting up, according to Classic Trader, which also warns of seized calipers on little-used cars and labour-intensive wiring repairs.

How much is a Lamborghini Miura worth?

Miura values have climbed steeply. In January 2007 WebCars said a rough P400 cost about $200,000 and good SVs up to about $800,000. Sotheby's lists results including a P400 at $995,000 at RM Sotheby's Monterey in 2022, a P400 at €1,608,125 at Villa Erba in 2023, and a 1969 P400 S at $1,897,000 at Monterey in August 2024.

SVs sell for far more. Sotheby's records a 1972 SV formerly owned by Jamiroquai's Jay Kay at $4.9 million at RM Sotheby's Dare to Dream sale in spring 2024, then a record. Carscoops reported in April 2026 that a 1971 SV sold for $4.46 million at Broad Arrow in 2025 and that a red low-mileage 1971 SV fetched almost $4.5 million at RM Sotheby's earlier in 2026. The current public record, reported by Supercars.net, is $6,605,000 for a largely original 1972 SV with under 18,300 miles at Broad Arrow's Amelia auction in 2026.
