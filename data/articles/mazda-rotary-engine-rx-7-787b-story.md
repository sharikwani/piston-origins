## An engine with no pistons

The Mazda rotary engine has no pistons. It drinks fuel, it burns oil, and it nearly bankrupted the company that built it. It also won the world's most famous race on what was very nearly its last chance, powered the RX-7, and in 2023 Mazda brought it back from the dead. For a channel called Piston Origins, it is an odd choice of hero, but few engines have a better story.

It begins in Hiroshima in 1961. Mazda, still trading under its earlier company name, was a small firm that mostly built trucks. Its president wanted something no other car maker had, and he found it in Germany: a strange engine invented by Felix Wankel, which Mazda licensed from NSU.

## How a Wankel rotary engine works

Instead of pistons pumping up and down, a Wankel rotary uses a triangle-shaped rotor spinning inside an oval housing. Intake, compression, power and exhaust all happen in one smooth rotation. That gives the rotary fewer moving parts, a tiny package and an appetite for revs.

The size matters as much as the smoothness. A rotary is so compact that it can sit low and far back in the car, which is excellent for handling. On paper it sounded perfect. There was just one problem: it kept destroying itself.

## The devil's nail marks and the apex seal fix

After only hours of running, the rotor's tips, known as the **apex seals**, chattered against the housing and carved scratches into its surface. Mazda's engineers had a name for them: the devil's nail marks.

Mazda handed the problem to a team of 47 young engineers led by Kenichi Yamamoto. In 1963 they tried a seal with cross-shaped holes. Then came a new material, an aluminium-carbon composite developed with an outside partner. The scratches stopped. In a very real sense, the whole programme was saved by a tiny seal.

## Cosmo Sport and the 84-hour Nürburgring marathon

On 30 May 1967 Mazda launched the Cosmo Sport, the world's first mass-production car with a two-rotor engine. Buyers still didn't trust the technology, so Mazda went looking for proof. In 1968 it entered an 84-hour non-stop marathon at the Nürburgring. One Cosmo finished fourth overall out of 59 cars, after covering more than 9,700 km.

Then came 1973 and the oil crisis. A US government report criticised the rotary's fuel economy, and over the next three years Mazda's sales fell 53 per cent, with rotary sales down 83 per cent. The company came close to bankruptcy. Most manufacturers would have killed the engine there and then. Mazda doubled down instead: Yamamoto's team went back to work and doubled the rotary's fuel economy in three years.

## The Mazda RX-7 and the rebuild myth

In 1978 Mazda bet on a small, light sports car: the RX-7. It sold more than 140,000 cars in its first two years.

The RX-7 also carries a reputation that still follows every rotary: that it needs a rebuild every 50,000 miles. The truth sits in the middle. Rotaries burn a little oil by design, and the apex seals remain the weak point, so an engine that runs low on oil or is never revved can wear out early. Looked after properly, many go much further. The channel's verdict is "mostly myth", with a firm reminder to rotary owners to check their oil.

## Mazda 787B: the 1991 Le Mans win

The moment rotary fans still talk about came at Le Mans in 1991. New rules were about to end the rotary's racing career there for good, but they were delayed by one year, giving Mazda one last chance with the 787B. It used four rotors, made around 700 horsepower and screamed to 9,000 rpm.

On 23 June 1991, car number 55, with Johnny Herbert at the wheel, crossed the line after 362 laps. Mazda had won. It was the first Japanese manufacturer ever to win Le Mans outright, and it remains the only winner with a rotary engine. After that, the rotary was gone from Le Mans for ever.

## The FD RX-7 and the 13B-REW

On the road, the rotary built its masterpiece in 1992: the third-generation RX-7, known as the FD. It used a twin-rotor **13B-REW** with sequential twin turbos, one working at low revs and the second joining in at around 4,500 rpm. It made 255 horsepower in America, weighed about 1,300 kg, and wore a shape many still call one of the most beautiful of the 1990s.

The FD became a pop-culture icon too. In the anime Initial D, one of the main rivals drives a yellow FD. In Tokyo Drift, Han's orange and black RX-7 became one of the best-known movie cars of the 2000s. And in Gran Turismo, a whole generation learned what a rotary sounds like at full song.

## Why the rotary went silent, and the 2023 comeback

Popularity didn't save it in the showroom. By 1995 the FD cost nearly $38,000, and few mechanics understood rotaries. Mazda pulled it from America after 1995, with only around 14,000 sold there. In Japan the RX-7 lived on until 2002, and the RX-8 carried the rotary until 2012. Then the engine went silent. Today a really clean FD can be worth more than $50,000, more than it cost new.

Mazda never let go, though. On 22 June 2023 rotary production restarted in Hiroshima, this time with the engine working as a range-extending generator in the MX-30 R-EV. So why do people love an engine that drinks fuel and burns oil? Because it was never supposed to work, and Mazda made it work anyway.
