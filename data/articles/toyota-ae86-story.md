## A cheap Corolla that became a legend

In 1983, Toyota sold a cheap, boxy little Corolla and, almost by accident, built a legend. It was light, it was rear-wheel drive, and it had a twin-cam engine that loved to rev. It would go on to help start a whole motorsport.

That car was the Toyota AE86. It was never fast in a straight line, and it was never meant to be special. Yet it became a legend, and the reasons say a lot about what makes a car great to drive.

## The last rear-wheel-drive Corolla: Levin and Trueno

To understand the AE86, you have to understand the moment it was born. By 1983, front-wheel drive was taking over. It was cheaper to build, roomier inside and easier to drive in the rain. So Toyota moved its new Corolla to the front wheels.

The sporty coupes did not follow. The Corolla Levin and the Sprinter Trueno stayed on the older platform, with the engine in the front and power going to the back. In other words, the old car was the one that kept rear-wheel drive, and that turned out to matter enormously.

The two were essentially one car with two faces. The Levin had fixed headlights, while the Trueno had pop-up headlights.

## Why the AE86 is called Hachi-Roku

Inside Toyota, the car was known by its chassis code, AE86. In Japan, people shortened that to Hachi-Roku, Japanese for "eight-six". Two badges, two faces, one nickname.

## The 4A-GE engine: 1.6 litres that loved to rev

Under the bonnet sat the AE86's real secret, the 4A-GE. It was a 1.6-litre inline-four with twin cams and 16 valves, making about 130 horsepower in Japan and just 112 in America.

On paper, that is not fast. But the AE86 was light, balanced and eager. The sportiest versions weighed around 900 kilos, less than a ton, and the 4A-GE kept pulling all the way to 7,500 rpm. The whole car felt alive in the driver's hands.

In America it arrived as the Corolla GT-S, and it was priced like what it was, an economy car. In 1985 you could buy one new for $11,530. Most buyers walked straight past it. A few did not.

## Was the AE86 actually fast?

Mostly, no. In a straight line plenty of ordinary family cars would beat it. Car and Driver timed the Corolla GT-S at 9.5 seconds from 0 to 60 mph, hardly the figure of a performance legend.

So how did it beat faster cars on a mountain road? It was fast where it counted, in the corners. Low weight, rear-wheel drive and an engine that rewarded high revs meant a skilled driver could carry speed through a bend that more powerful cars could not match.

## Keiichi Tsuchiya, the Drift King

That brings the story to the mountains of Japan and a young driver named Keiichi Tsuchiya. He learned his craft on the touge, the twisting mountain passes, throwing his car sideways through corners at speeds that made no sense.

His street driving videos made him famous and got him into serious trouble with the racing authorities; he nearly lost his licence. He also became known as the Drift King. Tsuchiya went on to race the AE86 in the All-Japan Touring Car Championship, finished second overall at Le Mans in 1999, and helped turn drifting from a street habit into a real motorsport.

## Initial D, the Takumi tax and the Toyota 86

Then, in 1995, a manga changed everything. Initial D, by Shuichi Shigeno, told the story of a teenage tofu delivery driver who wins downhill races in his father's white and black Sprinter Trueno. Tsuchiya even worked on the anime as a consultant. A tofu delivery car beating supercars was an idea a whole generation fell in love with.

Fans have a name for what happened to prices next: the Takumi tax, after the story's hero. A 1985 Corolla GT-S that cost $11,530 new sold at auction in 2021 for $40,000. For a Corolla, that sounds absurd, but once you know the story it makes sense.

Toyota noticed. In 2012 it launched a brand-new rear-wheel-drive coupe, built with Subaru and sold alongside the Subaru BRZ, and named it simply the 86. Light, balanced and affordable, it was a direct nod to the Hachi-Roku.

The AE86 endures because it proved power is not everything. A boxy little Corolla that was never meant to be special taught the world to drive sideways.
