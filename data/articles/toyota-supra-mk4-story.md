## The Mk4 Supra: a legend more than 30 years on

Some cars are fast. Some cars are famous. And then there is the one people still call the legend. Toyota built the Mk4 Supra, the A80, more than 30 years ago, and tuners are still pushing it past 1,000 horsepower. How did a Japanese sports coupé from 1993 become one of the most wanted cars on the planet? To understand it, you have to go back to the cars that came before.

## Where the Supra name came from: the 2000GT and the Celica Supra

In 1967 Toyota built the 2000GT, a long-nosed beauty that proved a Japanese company could build a world-class sports car. It is the Supra's spiritual ancestor, and its influence would show up again decades later.

Then, in 1978, Toyota took its Celica, stretched the nose, dropped in a straight-six and gave it a new name: Supra. It is Latin for "above", or "to go beyond". By 1986 the Supra stood on its own, with a turbocharged 3-litre straight-six. But Toyota wanted something more.

## Building the A80: Isao Tsuzuki's "less is more"

In February 1989 work began on the fourth-generation Supra, led by chief engineer **Isao Tsuzuki**, who had already worked on the first Celica and the MR2. His rule was simple: less is more. The new car had to be shorter, lower, wider and lighter, even while carrying more safety equipment.

So the team went to war on weight. The Mk4 got an aluminium bonnet, aluminium suspension arms, a plastic fuel tank and a magnesium steering-wheel frame. They even used hollow fibres in the carpet. Every gram counted, and the finished car was about 100 kg lighter than the one it replaced.

The shape followed the same thinking. Its flowing lines owed more to the 2000GT of the 1960s than to the boxy Supra that came immediately before it, which is why the A80 still looks so different from its predecessor.

## The 1993 launch: targa roof, hoop wing and huge brakes

The new Supra debuted at the Chicago Auto Show in 1993, and car lovers fell for the details. There was that huge hoop wing, a removable targa roof and double-wishbone suspension at all four corners. The brakes were so good that Car and Driver stopped one from 70 mph in just 149 feet. Zero to 60 mph took about four and a half seconds.

Those numbers made the Supra a serious performance car straight out of the box. But the reason it became a legend sits under the bonnet.

## The 2JZ-GTE engine and its sequential twin turbos

The heart of the Mk4 Supra Turbo is the **2JZ-GTE**: 3 litres, six cylinders in a row, a cast-iron block and two turbochargers developed with Hitachi. The clever part is that they work in sequence. Low in the rev range, one turbo does the work so the engine responds quickly. Higher up, the second joins in for the big rush.

In Japan the engine was rated at 280 PS (about 276 hp), the ceiling set by a gentlemen's agreement between car makers. In America it was rated at 320 hp. Power went through Toyota's first six-speed manual gearbox, developed with Getrag.

That iron block was so overbuilt that tuners doubled the factory power on standard internals, and the wildest builds have gone past 1,000 horsepower. It is the main reason the 2JZ name still carries so much weight in tuning circles.

## Tuners, Hollywood and The Fast and the Furious

The tuning world had already discovered the Supra when, in 2001, an orange Supra in The Fast and the Furious turned it into a superstar. A car already loved in tuner circles was now famous far beyond them.

Its value followed. One of the cars from that film sold at auction in 2021 for $550,000. For a Supra.

## The legacy of the Toyota Supra Mk4

Sales of the A80 ended in America in 1998 and in Japan in 2002. But the Mk4 never really left. A light, carefully engineered body, an engine built far stronger than it needed to be and a starring role on screen combined to keep it at the top of enthusiasts' wish lists.

More than 30 years after Tsuzuki's team started cutting grams, it is still the car people dream about.
