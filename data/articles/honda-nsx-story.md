## Senna's verdict at Suzuka

Suzuka, 1989. The reigning Formula One world champion climbs out of Honda's secret new supercar and tells the engineers it is fragile. The driver was Ayrton Senna, then winning races in Honda-powered McLarens, and his verdict sent Honda back to work. Eight months later the car was 50% stiffer, and Ferrari had a problem.

That car became the original Honda NSX, the NA1, sold in America as the Acura NSX. Its promise sounds simple now, but at the time almost nobody believed it could be done: Ferrari speed with Honda reliability. Everyone said it could not be done. The story of how Honda did it runs from a Pininfarina concept car to a hand-built line in Tochigi, by way of the Nürburgring and a world champion's harsh verdict.

## From the Pininfarina HP-X concept to the NS-X

The NSX story begins in 1984 with the HP-X, a concept car designed by Pininfarina. Five years later, in February 1989, the production-bound car was revealed at the Chicago Auto Show as the NS-X, short for New Sports Car Experimental.

Two things set it apart. The first was its body. The NSX was built from aluminium, with an all-aluminium monocoque, making it the first production car built this way. Compared with steel, the aluminium construction saved nearly 200 kilos.

The second was the cabin. The designers studied the cockpit of an F-16 fighter jet and borrowed its thinking: the cabin was pushed forward, with glass all around.

## Eight months to fix "fragile": Senna and the Nürburgring

Then Honda handed a prototype to the one driver who would notice every flaw. Between Formula One sessions at Suzuka, Senna drove the NSX and called it fragile. For a company whose engines were powering his championship-winning McLaren, that was not a comment to ignore.

Honda spent another eight months developing the car. Part of that work happened at the Nürburgring, where test driver Motoharu Kurosawa reported every flex in the chassis and engineers hand-welded new braces to cure it. By the end, the structure was around 50% stiffer than the one Senna had criticised.

## The C30A VTEC V6 and the Tochigi factory

The engine changed late in development too. The 3.0-litre V6, known as the C30A, was redesigned to use VTEC. It was also fitted with titanium connecting rods, a first for a production car, and it revved to 8,000 rpm.

How the NSX was built mattered as much as what went into it. Every car was assembled in Tochigi by a team of about 200 hand-picked technicians, each with at least ten years of experience.

## The Acura NSX in America and Gordon Murray's verdict

In November 1990 the car arrived in the United States as the Acura NSX, priced at around $60,000. It handled like a race car and started every morning like a Honda, which was exactly the point. Ferrari speed and Honda reliability had been treated as opposites; the NSX put them in the same car.

Its influence reached well beyond its sales. Gordon Murray, the designer of the McLaren F1, called it a landmark car, one that woke up not only a lazy Ferrari but Porsche as well. Honda kept building the first-generation NSX for 15 years, from 1990 until 2005, and made just over 18,000 cars in that time.

## What an original Honda NSX is worth today

The $60,000 Acura has aged well as an investment. Clean examples now sell for roughly $80,000 to $120,000, and one low-mileage 1991 car sold at auction for $155,911.

Senna called it fragile, and Honda answered by turning it into the supercar that changed supercars. Its lasting lesson is a simple one: the best supercar might be the one you can drive every day.
