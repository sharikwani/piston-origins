title: Nissan GT-R vs Porsche 911 Turbo: 2008 Nürburgring Dispute
description: In 2008 Porsche bought a Nissan GT-R R35, lapped the Nürburgring in 7:54 against Nissan's claimed 7:29 and suggested race tyres. Here is how Nissan answered.
---
## The Porsche 911 Turbo and the everyday supercar

For years, if you wanted an everyday supercar, the default answer was the Porsche 911 Turbo. Then Nissan arrived with the R35 GT-R, a Japanese car costing roughly half as much. In the US, the 2009 GT-R was priced at $69,850, while the 2010 Porsche 911 Turbo started at $132,800.

In 2008 that gap turned into one of the stranger rows in car history. Porsche bought a Nissan GT-R, flew it to Germany, drove it around the Nürburgring and then suggested Nissan had cheated. Nissan's reply is still quoted today.

## Godzilla and the hand-built VR38DETT engine

The GT-R's nickname, Godzilla, goes back to 1989, when Australian journalists at Wheels magazine first gave it to an earlier GT-R. The R35's chief engineer, Kazutoshi Mizuno, built the car around a hand-built twin-turbo V6, the 3.8-litre VR38DETT, rated at 480 PS.

Each engine is assembled by a single craftsman from start to finish, and that builder's name goes on a plaque on the engine. These engine builders are known as Takumi, and only nine people held that job. It was a deliberate signal that this was not simply a fast, cheap alternative to a Porsche but a carefully made machine in its own right.

## Nissan's 7:29 Nürburgring Nordschleife lap

To prove the car, Nissan went where every manufacturer goes to make its point: the Nürburgring Nordschleife, around 20 km long with more than 150 corners. Test driver Toshio Suzuki set a lap of 7 minutes 29 seconds. Nissan said that was three seconds faster than Porsche's own 911 GT2.

A car at half the price beating Porsche on the track Porsche treats as home ground was always going to provoke a response from Stuttgart. The Nordschleife is where reputations are built, and a lap time quoted from it carries weight in showrooms and magazine pages alike. Nissan had not just claimed parity with the 911 Turbo; it had claimed to beat the more extreme GT2.

## Porsche's test: GT-R 7:54 vs 911 Turbo 7:38

Porsche bought a US-specification GT-R for around $77,000, flew it to Germany and put its own drivers in it. Their best lap in the Nissan was 7:54, a full 25 seconds slower than Nissan's claim. Porsche's own 911 Turbo did 7:38 in the same test, and its GT2 went quicker still at 7:34.

Set those numbers side by side and the problem is obvious. In Porsche's hands, the GT-R was 16 seconds behind the 911 Turbo. In Nissan's hands, it was quicker than the GT2. Both could not be the whole story, and 25 seconds is a very large margin on a single lap, even one as long as the Nordschleife.

Porsche's conclusion was that Nissan must have run its record lap on special semi-slick race tyres. In other words, it was accusing Nissan of cheating. The head of the 911 programme, August Achleitner, tried to soften the blow, saying that the Nissan was a good car and that he did not want to say anything bad about it.

## Nissan's reply: standard tyres and driving lessons

Nissan did not back down. Neil Reeve of Nissan Europe said the lap had been done on absolutely standard tyres available to customers in the showroom. Nissan offered to show Porsche the actual tyres and the video of the run.

Then came the line that made the story famous. In its statement, Nissan said any manufacturer buying a GT-R should follow the car's run-in procedure, and pointed out that it offers performance driving courses to GT-R owners. Nobody needed the subtext spelled out: perhaps Porsche's drivers were the ones who needed lessons.

It was an unusually sharp public exchange between two car makers. Manufacturers argue about lap times all the time, but rarely does one buy the other's car, publish its own numbers and imply foul play, and rarely does the other answer by questioning the rival's driving.

## Who was right, Porsche or Nissan?

Porsche never proved that Nissan cheated, and Nissan never changed its story. What Nissan did instead was keep making the GT-R faster around the Nordschleife, with later laps of 7:26.70, 7:24.22 and 7:19.1.

The most emphatic answer came in 2013, when the GT-R Nismo, with Michael Krumm driving, lapped the Nordschleife in 7:08.679. Whatever happened with Porsche's own test car in 2008, the R35 had shown it could go far quicker than the 7:29 that started the argument.

The dispute still splits enthusiasts. Porsche's numbers came from a real car bought off the street, driven by its own people. Nissan's came from the company that built the car, on tyres it insisted anyone could buy. The two sides never settled it, and the GT-R's later lap times did most of Nissan's arguing for it.
