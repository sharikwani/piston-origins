## Toyota Team Europe and the Celica GT-Four

In 1995 Toyota was not the underdog of the World Rally Championship. It was the team everyone else was chasing. Its rally operation was not even run from Japan: it worked out of Cologne in Germany as Toyota Team Europe, and its weapon was the Celica GT-Four, a turbocharged, four-wheel-drive car built for mud, snow and gravel.

The record behind it was formidable. In 1990 Carlos Sainz won the drivers' world title in a Celica GT-Four, the first time a Japanese car had done it. In 1993 Toyota became the first Japanese manufacturer to win the world title, and in 1994 it won again. By 1995 the latest car, the Celica GT-Four ST205 with its 2.0-litre 3S-GTE turbo four, was carrying the weight of all that success.

## The 1995 WRC turbo restrictor rule

That season the rule book had one job: slow these cars down. Every turbo engine had to breathe through a restrictor, a metal ring with a small hole placed right in front of the turbocharger. The effect is like making a sprinter run while breathing through a straw. Less air means less power, and the limit worked out at about 300 horsepower. Every car was checked.

On paper, the new Celica was struggling. It had won just one rally all year, in Corsica. And yet, with two rounds left, Toyota was leading both world championships.

## Suspicion at Rally Australia

The first public doubts surfaced at Rally Australia, on a short show stage in a park in Perth where two cars race side by side. Rivals watched the Celicas leave the line and pull away from the Subarus. Juha Kankkunen's co-driver, Nicky Grist, later suggested that was the mistake: showing that kind of speed on live television. People started asking questions.

## Rally Catalunya, October 1995

The next round was Rally Catalunya in Spain, round seven of eight. Kankkunen, already a four-time world champion, took the lead on stage two and built a gap of almost a minute. Then, on day two, he found a left-hander tighter than he expected, ran out of road and rolled the Celica out of the rally. Team-mate Didier Auriol kept going and finished fourth, so Toyota's title hopes were still alive.

Then the scrutineers took Auriol's car apart. They pulled off a hose and checked the turbo, and everything looked legal. Hours later, Auriol was thrown out: his turbo restrictor did not match the rule book.

## How the turbo restrictor cheat worked

The restrictor itself was perfectly legal. Remove it, measure it, and it passed. The trick was hidden in the air hose around it. Inside was a flange held shut by a strong spring. When mechanics clamped the hose in place, the clips forced that flange open by just 5 mm.

Five millimetres sounds trivial, but it let air sneak around the restrictor and straight into the turbo. That was roughly 25 per cent more air, worth up to 50 extra horsepower on cars meant to be limited to 300.

The clever part was what happened under inspection. The moment an inspector undid the clips to check the system, the spring snapped the flange shut, and the evidence erased itself. Even the springs were polished and machined so they would not disturb the airflow, and opening the device again needed a special tool. The diagrams usually used to explain it are simplified, but the principle is that simple: a legal part, bypassed by a hose that only cheated while it was fitted to the car.

## The FIA ban: Toyota excluded from the 1995 championship

On 3 November 1995 the FIA's World Motor Sport Council met in an emergency session. Toyota Team Europe was excluded from the 1995 championship and every point erased. Kankkunen, Auriol and Armin Schwarz lost everything they had scored, and the team was banned for 12 months, covering the whole of the 1996 season.

With Toyota gone, the drivers' title went to a 27-year-old Scot in a blue Subaru, Colin McRae, and Subaru took the manufacturers' title too.

The most surprising reaction came from the man who handed down the punishment. FIA president Max Mosley described the device as the most ingenious thing he had seen in 30 years of motorsport, and called the parts inside "beautifully made". He also said there was nothing to suggest the drivers knew. Toyota's position was that the device had been made at a certain level of the team without management knowing. The FIA's answer was blunt: a team is responsible for everything it does.

Some questions remain open. Who designed the device has never been publicly named, and even how the FIA found it is still debated.

## Toyota's WRC comeback

The ban was not the end of Toyota in rallying. In 1999 its Corolla WRC won the manufacturers' world title. Toyota later left the sport, then returned in 2017 with the Yaris. As of 2026 it holds 10 manufacturers' titles, level with Lancia for the most in the championship's history.

That is the strange arc of this story: the team behind the cleverest cheat rallying has seen went on to become one of the most successful it has ever had. Whether the Celica ST205's hidden hose was genius or simply cheating is still the argument it was in 1995.
