## What is the 2JZ-GTE?

The 2JZ-GTE is Toyota's 3.0-litre, twin-turbocharged inline six-cylinder engine, best known as the engine in the fourth-generation (A80) Supra Turbo. It belongs to the JZ family of inline-sixes that Toyota started in 1990 with the 2.5-litre 1JZ. The code describes the engine: JZ is the family, G marks the performance twin-cam head, T means turbocharged and E means electronic fuel injection.

It displaces 2,997 cc from a square bore and stroke of 86 mm by 86 mm. The block is cast iron, the cylinder head is aluminium with twin overhead camshafts and four valves per cylinder, and the camshafts are driven by a belt. HP Academy lists the compression ratio as 8.5:1, against 10:1 for the naturally aspirated 2JZ-GE, with the lower figure coming from recessed piston crowns.

In Japan the engine was rated at 280 PS (about 276 hp), the ceiling set by the domestic manufacturers' gentlemen's agreement. The US-market Supra Turbo, launched for 1993, was rated at 320 hp at 5,600 rpm and 315 lb-ft at 4,000 rpm, according to Jalopnik.

## 2JZ-GTE history: where did it come from?

The 2JZ replaced the 3.0-litre 7M-GTE that had powered the A70 Supra and the Z20 Soarer. HP Academy says Toyota's aim was an engine stronger and more reliable than its predecessor that could answer the Nissan RB26DETT, which had arrived in the R32 Skyline GT-R in 1989. The naturally aspirated 2JZ-GE is essentially a stroked version of the 1JZ-GE, and the GTE added turbocharging, intercooling and its own cylinder head.

The 2JZ-GTE first went on sale in 1991 in the Toyota Aristo (JZS147), a Japan-market saloon. Jalopnik reports that it was introduced there to homologate the Aristo for the Japanese Grand Touring Car Championship; the engine became famous two years later, when it went into the JZA80 Supra in 1993.

Toyota added VVT-i variable valve timing on the intake camshaft in 1997 (Wikipedia gives September 1997; HP Academy says mid-1997). With it, the Japanese torque rating rose from 44.3 kg·m (434 Nm) to 46 kg·m (451 Nm). US buyers never received the VVT-i engine: 1998 was the last model year for the Supra in America. Production in the Supra ended in 2002, and the VVT-i engine continued in the Aristo (JZS161) until 2005.

## Why is the 2JZ-GTE so strong?

The reputation rests on the bottom end. The block is cast iron with a fully closed deck, meaning the top of the block is solid around the cylinders rather than open to the water jacket, which supports the bores under high cylinder pressure. The crankshaft is forged steel with 12 counterweights and runs in seven main bearings, each held by two bolts. Jalopnik also lists forged connecting rods and oil-cooled cast aluminium pistons, and notes that the complete engine weighs more than 500 pounds.

The turbocharged version adds oil spray nozzles beneath the pistons to cool them, a feature the 2JZ-GE lacks. In a teardown reported by Motorious, drag racer Stephan Papadakis pointed to the multi-layer steel head gasket, a girdle bolted to the underside of the block to stiffen the main bearing area, and wide bearings with a generous thrust bearing.

The engine was developed during Japan's bubble economy, and Jalopnik credits the iron block, main bearings, crankshaft, closed deck, rods and pistons together for its durability under heavy modification. In practice, the block and crankshaft are rarely the first parts to fail in a modified 2JZ-GTE.

## Turbochargers and market differences

The 2JZ-GTE uses two turbochargers in sequence rather than in parallel. At low engine speed one turbocharger does all the work for quicker response; at higher revs the second is brought in. Wikipedia says the system was developed by Toyota with Hitachi.

Japanese and export engines differ in several details. Japanese cars used CT20 turbochargers with ceramic turbine wheels, 440 cc injectors and a MAP (manifold pressure) load sensor. Export engines, including US cars, used CT12B turbochargers with steel turbine wheels, 550 cc injectors and a mass airflow meter. HP Academy also records slightly different camshaft specifications between the two. In the Supra, the engine was paired with either the six-speed V160 manual gearbox, built with Getrag, or the four-speed A340E automatic.

## Which cars had the 2JZ-GTE?

From the factory, the 2JZ-GTE was fitted to only three models: the Toyota Aristo 3.0V (JZS147), the Toyota Aristo V300 (JZS161) and the Toyota Supra RZ and Turbo (JZA80). The related non-turbo 2JZ-GE went into a much wider range, including the Lexus GS300, SC300 and IS300, the Toyota Crown, Mark II, Chaser and Cresta, and the non-turbo Supra.

That distinction matters for buyers and engine swappers. A Lexus with a 3.0-litre inline-six has a 2JZ, but not the turbocharged 2JZ-GTE, and the two engines differ in pistons, cylinder head, oil cooling and fuelling.

## How much power can a 2JZ-GTE make?

Power figures for the 2JZ-GTE circulate widely, and most come from tuners rather than controlled tests. Jalopnik reports that most tuners consider the stock bottom end good for around 800 hp. HP Academy gives a more conservative view: it treats about 600 hp as a sensible limit for an engine that will be driven hard and often on standard internals, recommends aftermarket main bearing caps from around 800 hp, and describes a standard engine run at 1,000 hp as a "dyno queen" with a much shortened life.

The common recipe for those numbers, according to Jalopnik, replaces the sequential turbochargers with one large single turbocharger, adds a front-mounted intercooler, larger injectors and upgraded fuel lines, and changes the intake and exhaust. HP Academy notes that the factory engine computer cannot be reflashed, so serious tuning needs a standalone or piggyback ECU. With aftermarket internals in a standard block, HP Academy says outputs well beyond 2,000 hp are possible.

## 2JZ-GTE problems and known weaknesses

The weak points are mostly around the engine rather than inside the block. HP Academy lists the sequential turbocharger system, whose changeover from one turbo to two is a common source of trouble, and the ceramic turbine wheels of Japanese-market turbochargers, which do not tolerate extra boost well. Valve stem seals tend to wear early, causing oil smoke at start-up, and the factory coil packs often fail as boost rises.

Inside the engine, the main bearing caps are usually the first part to give up on a highly tuned standard motor, and HP Academy says the cylinder head can crack in drag cars running five- and six-second quarter-miles. Motorious notes that the bucket-style lifters limit how aggressive camshaft profiles can be. VVT-i engines also need their cam timing tuned, which adds complexity.

## 2JZ-GTE parts supply today

Because the engine has been out of production for two decades, parts support matters. HP Academy reports that Toyota still sells new VVT-i blocks and cylinder heads, but not the earlier non-VVT-i versions. In January 2020, Toyota's GR Heritage Parts project announced that it would remake parts for the A80 Supra, starting with items such as headlamps, door handles and the brake booster, for sale in Japan, North America and Europe.
