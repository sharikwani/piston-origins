## What is the Honda C30A?

The C30A is the 3.0-litre, naturally aspirated V6 that Honda developed for the original NSX, the mid-engined sports car launched in September 1990. Honda's own engine history describes it as an engine built exclusively for the NSX, and Car Throttle notes that neither the C30A nor its 3.2-litre successor was fitted as standard to any other Honda.

It displaces 2,977 cc from a bore and stroke of 90.0 mm by 78.0 mm, runs a 10.2:1 compression ratio and has twin overhead camshafts per bank, belt-driven, with four valves per cylinder. The banks sit at 90 degrees. In Japan, Honda rated the manual-gearbox engine at 280 PS at 7,300 rpm and 30.0 kgm (about 294 Nm) at 5,400 rpm, with an 8,000 rpm redline. North American cars were rated at 270 hp; MotoIQ quotes the factory figures as 270 hp at 7,100 rpm and 210 lb-ft at 5,300 rpm, while evo gives 270 bhp at 7,300 rpm. The small differences reflect different markets and rating methods rather than different hardware.

Cars with the four-speed automatic used a milder version. Honda lists it at 265 PS at 6,800 rpm with a 7,500 rpm redline, and US-market automatic cars were rated at 252 hp.

## C30A history: how Honda developed the NSX engine

Honda's account of the project explains most of the engine's character. The company wanted an engine that would rev high without strain and respond directly to the throttle, and it wanted that performance to be usable by an ordinary owner rather than only a racing driver. It rejected turbocharging because of the added weight and size, the heat management it demanded and the lag of turbos of the period.

The engine sits transversely behind the cabin, tilted five degrees towards the rear, with the gearbox alongside it. Honda considered other V angles: a 120-degree V was too wide for a transverse installation and a 60-degree V too tall. It settled on the 90-degree layout already used in the V6 of the 1985 Legend, whose 2.0-litre C20A and 2.5-litre C25A were the basis of early NSX plans. Those Legend engines had single-cam heads.

The decisive change came in 1989, when Honda launched the Integra with VTEC, which the company describes as the world's first variable valve timing and lift system. Shortly afterwards the NSX engine was switched from single-cam heads to double-overhead-cam VTEC. Evo puts it plainly: VTEC was fitted late on.

## C30A design details: VTEC, intake and titanium rods

The VTEC system gives each cylinder a third intake rocker arm and cam lobe. At low and medium engine speeds the two outer lobes work the intake valves, with the secondary valve opening later and closing earlier to create swirl. At 5,800 rpm on manual cars the centre high-lift lobe takes over. Honda lists intake lift as 8.7 mm and 8.3 mm on the low-speed lobes and 10.2 mm on the high-lift lobe. Evo describes the change-over as happening at about 6,000 rpm.

To fill the torque dip between cam profiles, Honda fitted a variable-volume resonance intake. Six shutter valves inside the V close at lower revs so the plenum works by resonance, then open at 4,800 rpm to use inertia effects; above 5,800 rpm the high-lift cams take over. Owners often call this system VVIS.

The best-known detail is the connecting rods. The C30A used titanium rods, which Car Throttle and other sources describe as a first for a production car. Honda says the rods are made from a Ti-3Al-2V alloy with a chromium nitride coating, weigh about 190 g less than steel equivalents (roughly 30 per cent) and raised the maximum engine speed by about 700 rpm.

Other weight-saving measures included an open-deck aluminium block with cast-in iron liners, aluminium cylinder heads, magnesium intake chambers and head covers, and hollow exhaust camshafts. The engine used pent-roof combustion chambers with central spark plugs, two knock sensors and direct ignition with a coil on each plug instead of a distributor. Honda says the Japanese engine was designed for RON 100 fuel.

## C30A variants and the move to 3.2 litres

The C30A stayed in production with few headline changes. For the NSX Type R of November 1992, Honda tightened quality control on crankshaft balance and on the weight matching of pistons and connecting rods.

In 1997 the manual NSX moved to the 3.2-litre C32B. Honda's Japanese engine history explains that the cast-iron liners were replaced with fibre-reinforced metal, which cut the wall between cylinders from 10 mm to 7 mm and allowed a larger bore. The C32B kept the 78 mm stroke, grew the bore to 93 mm and displaced 3,179 cc. Car Throttle gives the US rating as 290 hp at 7,100 rpm and 221 lb-ft at 5,500 rpm, with a six-speed manual gearbox. NSX production ended in December 2005.

## Cars fitted with the C30A

The only road car fitted with the C30A as standard was the first-generation NSX, sold as the Honda NSX in Japan and Europe and as the Acura NSX in North America. The manual versions used the C30A from 1990 until the 1997 switch to the C32B, and automatic cars used the lower-output C30A tune.

## C30A reliability, maintenance and tuning

The engine's reputation for durability is strong. Car Throttle's used-buying guide calls the motors "reliable as tax day" and reports that Honda never had a single warranty claim on the VTEC system, though that is the publication's claim rather than published Honda data. Evo notes that Hondas are known for oil-tight engines, so any leak deserves suspicion, and that if power does not build sharply as revs rise, the VTEC or the variable intake may be at fault. JDMBUYSELL, citing MotorTrend's buyer's guide, lists the valve-cover gaskets, rear camshaft plugs and VTEC solenoids as places to check for oil leaks.

The cambelt is the main service item, and published intervals differ. Car Throttle says the timing belt and water pump should be replaced every 90,000 miles. Evo, writing for UK buyers, recommends every five years or 60,000 miles and strongly advises replacing the camshaft pulley at the same time, warning that a slipping belt causes poor running and a broken one can damage the valves.

The C30A is rarely tuned heavily. Car Throttle describes the aftermarket as limited and expensive and suggests an exhaust as the first step. MotoIQ's Project NSX series tested a ported intake manifold and headers on a naturally aspirated C30A, and one reader with a modified intake reported just under 270 hp at the wheels, an anecdotal figure rather than a controlled test.

## What is a C30A-powered NSX worth today?

Because the engine was only ever sold in the NSX, its value is the car's value. In the UK, evo's used guide says early cars start at around £60,000 and the best low-mileage later cars reach about £150,000. Evo also counted roughly 250 NSXs in the UK in 2025, more than 150 of them declared off the road.
