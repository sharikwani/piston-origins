25-year import rule

The 25-year import rule is the US federal exemption that lets a motor vehicle at least 25 years old be imported without meeting the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards (FMVSS). It sits in US law at 49 U.S.C. 30112(b)(9). The rule is the flip side of the Imported Vehicle Safety Compliance Act of 1988, which cracked down on "grey market" cars brought in through unofficial channels; Kiplinger's counted 66,879 such imports in 1985 alone. Because newer non-compliant cars are effectively shut out, Japanese-market models never sold in America, such as the Skyline GT-R, only become importable as they pass their 25th birthday.

Aluminium monocoque

An aluminium monocoque is a car body in which the aluminium shell itself carries the loads, instead of being bolted to a separate frame. The 1990 Honda NSX is credited by Honda as the world's first production car with an all-aluminium monocoque body. Honda chose it to save weight: the body was 140 kg lighter than a steel equivalent, and the complete car nearly 200 kg lighter. The NSX was built in a dedicated plant in Tochigi designed around skilled workers rather than a conveyor line. Evo calls it the first series-production aluminium monocoque, "a decade ahead of Ferrari".

Apex seal

An apex seal is the thin strip at each of the three tips of a Wankel rotary engine's rotor that seals against the housing, doing the job piston rings do in a conventional engine. Without a good seal at each tip, the engine loses compression and power. Early Mazda rotaries used 6 mm carbon seals that wore quickly and could mark the housing; later engines used 2 mm three-piece cast-iron seals that last much longer in standard form. Typical causes of failure are poor lubrication, overheating and detonation. Evo notes that Mazda never fully cured tip-seal wear, so most RX-7s eventually need an engine rebuild.

Sources: Jalopnik · evo

ATTESA E-TS

ATTESA E-TS is Nissan's computer-controlled four-wheel-drive system, best known from the Skyline GT-R. The name stands for Advanced Total Traction Engineering System for All-Terrain with Electronic Torque Split. In normal driving it sends all the power to the rear wheels, so the car behaves like a rear-wheel-drive sports car, and when the rear starts to lose grip it can send up to half of the torque to the front axle. On the R32 Skyline GT-R it worked alongside the RB26DETT twin-turbo engine and HICAS rear-wheel steering.

Sources: evo

Boost

Boost is the extra air pressure a turbocharger or supercharger forces into an engine's intake, above normal atmospheric pressure. It is usually measured in psi or bar (1 bar is about 14.5 psi). More pressure packs more air, and so more fuel, into each cylinder, which makes more power. Jalopnik puts a typical stock turbo car at 6 to 8 psi, while the Ferrari F80 runs about 55.5 psi. The real limit is how much strain the pistons, rods and cylinder head can take. On a turbo engine, a valve called the wastegate caps boost by letting some exhaust gas bypass the turbine.

Bore and stroke

Bore and stroke are the two basic measurements of a piston engine: bore is the diameter of each cylinder, and stroke is how far the piston travels from the top to the bottom of the cylinder. Together with the number of cylinders, they set the engine's displacement. An engine with a bore larger than its stroke is called oversquare; it has room for bigger valves and keeps piston speeds lower, which suits high revs. Equal figures make it "square". The Toyota 2JZ-GTE is square at 86 mm by 86 mm, while the Nissan RB26DETT is oversquare at 86 mm by 73.7 mm.

Carbon-fibre monocoque

A carbon-fibre monocoque is a car's main structure moulded from carbon-fibre-reinforced plastic (CFRP), a material that is very stiff and light, with the shell itself carrying the loads. McLaren's MP4/1 of 1981 was the first Formula 1 car with a chassis made entirely of carbon composite, and it won the 1981 British Grand Prix. McLaren calls its later F1 road car the world's first fully composite road car. The Lexus LFA used CFRP for 65% of its body-in-white, saving around 100 kg compared with an equivalent aluminium body.

Chassis code

A chassis code is the manufacturer's internal model code that identifies a specific version of a car, and enthusiasts often use it as the car's name. Toyota's AE86 is the classic example. The first letter, A, identifies the engine family (the A-series); E stands for the Corolla model family; 8 marks the fifth-generation Corolla; and 6 marks the high-performance, rear-wheel-drive coupe derivative. The code became so famous that Japanese fans nicknamed the car Hachi-Roku, "eight-six", and it is better known by that code than by its badge.

Drifting

Drifting is a driving style, and now a judged motorsport, in which the driver deliberately makes the rear wheels slide and holds the car sideways through a corner under control. The FIA says it began on Japan's mountain passes (tōge) in the 1980s. Keiichi Tsuchiya, nicknamed the "Drift King" by a Car Boy magazine editor who saw him sliding in the rain near Fuji Speedway, made his name in the rear-wheel-drive AE86. The D1 Grand Prix series, founded by Isao Saita, has run since 2001, and in 2017 the FIA set unified world regulations for the sport.

Sources: FIA · Toyota Global

Gentlemen's agreement

The gentlemen's agreement was an informal pact among Japanese car makers to advertise no more than 280 PS (about 276 hp) for cars sold in Japan. It began in 1989 under the Japan Automobile Manufacturers Association, against a background of high road deaths, and was not legally binding. It is why cars such as the Skyline GT-R, Supra, RX-7 and NSX were quoted at about 276 hp, though many were widely believed to make more. Evo describes the R34's 276 bhp as "a ceiling agreed between car makers in Japan". The limit was abandoned in 2004, when the Honda Legend became the first car to officially exceed it.

Getrag

Getrag is a German maker of car transmissions, known to Skyline fans for the six-speed manual gearbox in the R34 GT-R. Evo singles out "the shift of the six-speed Getrag gearbox" as a highlight of the R34 and notes that the drivetrain is rated for far more torque than the standard car makes. Magna International bought the Getrag group in the first quarter of 2016, describing it as one of the world's leading independent suppliers of automotive transmissions and a leader in dual-clutch gearboxes.

Group A

Group A was the production-based category that ran the World Rally Championship from 1987, after Group B was scrapped. The day after Henri Toivonen and Sergio Cresto were killed in Corsica in 1986, FISA president Jean-Marie Balestre announced that the 1987 championship would be for Group A cars only. To homologate a car, a maker had to build 5,000 a year, a figure that had fallen to 2,500 by 1994. An airflow restrictor, introduced in 1990, held turbo cars to around 300 bhp. Group A gave the world road cars such as the Toyota Celica GT-Four, and was replaced by the World Rally Car formula in 1997.

Sources: Goodwood Road & Racing · evo

Group B

Group B was the rally category of the early-to-mid 1980s whose loose rules produced the fastest and most dangerous rally cars ever built. A maker needed to build only 200 examples within 12 months to homologate a car, half the previous Group 4 figure, and could then add a new 20-car "evolution" every year. Power soared: Evo puts Audi's S1 E2 Quattro at 550 bhp or more. After Henri Toivonen and co-driver Sergio Cresto died when their Lancia Delta S4 crashed on the Tour de Corse on 2 May 1986, Group B was banned from the end of that season.

Hachi-Roku

Hachi-Roku is Japanese for "eight-six" and is the nickname of the Toyota AE86, the rear-wheel-drive Corolla Levin and Sprinter Trueno coupés built from 1983 to 1987. Strictly, only cars with the 1.6-litre twin-cam 16-valve 4A-GEU engine carried the AE86 designation. The car's motorsport success and later drift culture turned it into a cult icon, and Toyota says its reputation grew further after it starred in a popular manga in the 1990s, Initial D.

HICAS

HICAS is Nissan's four-wheel-steering system; the name stands for High Capacity Actively Controlled Steering. It lets the rear wheels steer slightly as well as the fronts. Turning the rear wheels the opposite way to the fronts helps manoeuvring at low speed, while turning them the same way improves stability at high speed. It was one of the technologies, alongside ATTESA E-TS four-wheel drive, that made the R32 Skyline GT-R what Evo calls "a technological tour-de-force".

Homologation

Homologation is official approval of a car for sale or for competition; in motorsport, it means a race car is accepted as complying with a series' rules. The FIA defines it as "the process of granting approval by an official authority". Because many series required race cars to be based on production models, makers built "homologation specials": road cars made in just large enough numbers to qualify. Group B needed only 200 cars in 12 months, while the Group A Toyota Celica GT-Four ST185 needed 5,000 road cars. The modern Toyota GR Yaris is a recent example of the breed.

Initial D

Initial D is a Japanese street-racing manga by Shuichi Shigeno, serialised in Kodansha's Young Magazine from 1995 to 2013 and collected in 48 volumes. Its hero, Takumi Fujiwara, is a high-school student who makes late-night tofu deliveries on Mount Akina in his father's modified Toyota AE86 Sprinter Trueno, which turns him into the best amateur road racer on the mountain. Toyota itself credits a popular 1990s manga with enhancing the AE86's reputation, and the car is still closely tied to the series in car culture.

Intercooler

An intercooler is a radiator-like heat exchanger that cools the air leaving a turbocharger or supercharger before it enters the engine. Compressing air heats it, and hot air is less dense, so cooling the charge lets more air and fuel fit into each cylinder for more power. An air-to-air intercooler uses passing airflow, like a radiator; an air-to-water type, or charge cooler, uses its own coolant circuit, pump and radiator. If that coolant gets too hot, the intercooler loses effectiveness and power drops.

JDM

JDM stands for Japanese Domestic Market: strictly, cars, parts and equipment made for sale in Japan rather than for export. What matters is the market a car was built for, not where it was assembled, so a Japanese-market Nissan Silvia is JDM while its American cousin, the 240SX, is not. Among enthusiasts the term has broadened to mean Japanese performance cars, Japanese aftermarket parts and Japanese-inspired tuning styles in general, often including cars that are not technically JDM.

Kei car

A kei car is Japan's smallest class of road car, built to strict size and engine limits in return for lower taxes. Since 1998 the rules have capped kei cars at 3.40 m long, 1.48 m wide and 2.00 m tall, with an engine of 660 cc or less, and makers quote no more than 64 PS. When the class began, engines were limited to 360 cc. The flat-rate kei vehicle tax is ¥10,800, and kei cars took 34.9% of Japanese new-car sales in 2020.

Sources: Nippon.com · Goodwood Road & Racing

Limited-slip differential

A limited-slip differential (LSD) is a differential that still lets the driven wheels turn at different speeds through corners, but limits how much one wheel can spin faster than the other when grip runs out. An ordinary open differential sends power along the path of least resistance, so a spinning wheel takes most of it. An LSD balances the wheels until traction returns. Common types use clutch plates, viscous fluid or helical gears, and many modern cars control clutch pressure electronically. The V-spec R34 Skyline GT-R had an active rear differential.

Motorex

Motorex was a Gardena, California company, registered with the US safety regulator NHTSA as a Registered Importer, that brought Japanese-market Nissan Skylines into the United States by certifying they had been brought up to federal standards. NHTSA found that Motorex had "submitted false statements of conformity" for 17 Nissan GTS and GTR cars. The agency sent the company a show-cause letter on 30 June 2005 and revoked its registration on 15 February 2006.

Nürburgring Nordschleife lap times

A Nordschleife lap time is the time a car takes to complete one lap of the Nürburgring's Nordschleife ("north loop") in Germany, the 20.8 km circuit car makers use as a public benchmark of performance. Official record laps are timed over a full 20.832 km lap from a flying start, certified by a notary, with TÜV Rheinland checking that the car is in production condition. The Mercedes-AMG ONE set the production-car record of 6:29.090 on 23 September 2024. Nissan's GT-R Nismo lapped in 7:08.679 on 30 September 2013, which Nissan called the fastest for a volume production car.

Redline

The redline is the highest engine speed, in revolutions per minute (rpm), that the manufacturer allows, marked in red on the rev counter. A rev limiter, usually set at or just above it, cuts in to protect the engine. How high it sits depends on how light the pistons, rods and crankshaft are, and how light and stiff the valvetrain is. A 1989 Chevrolet Camaro with the L98 V8 redlines at 5,500 rpm; the Lexus LFA's 4.8-litre V10 at 9,000 rpm. The original Honda NSX's V6 has its red line at 8,000 rpm and its limiter at 8,300.

Sequential twin-turbo

A sequential twin-turbo system uses two turbochargers that work in turn rather than together. At low revs one turbo boosts all the cylinders on its own for quick response; at higher revs, valves redirect exhaust gas so the second turbo joins in for top-end power. The aim is to reduce turbo lag without giving up peak output, at the cost of extra complexity. The Porsche 959 used the idea in 1986. In Mazda's FD RX-7, the 13B-REW twin-rotor engine's second turbo starts contributing from around 4,500 rpm.

Shaken

Shaken is Japan's compulsory vehicle safety inspection. A new car needs its first shaken three years after purchase, then one every two years. Japan-guide puts a typical shaken at 100,000 to 200,000 yen, which covers the inspection fee, a weight tax of usually 8,000 to 50,000 yen and compulsory insurance of about 30,000 yen, before any repairs the inspection demands. That regular cost is a big part of what owning a car in Japan involves.

Sources: japan-guide.com

Show or Display

Show or Display is a US exemption that lets certain cars which do not meet federal safety standards be imported if they are of historical or technological significance. NHTSA's rule took effect on 13 August 1999 and is aimed mainly at models their makers never sold in the United States; rarity, unusual technology and links to historical figures all count. Owners must apply to NHTSA, and a car allowed on the road under the rule may cover no more than 2,500 miles in any 12-month period.

Takumi

Takumi is the Japanese word for a master craftsman, and car fans know it two ways. Nissan uses the title for "a master craftsman who has perfected his skills over years of painstaking work and dedication": at its Yokohama plant, each Nissan GT-R engine is hand-assembled by one takumi, who then fixes a plaque bearing his name to it. In Initial D, Takumi Fujiwara is the manga's hero, a teenage tofu-delivery driver in an AE86.

Turbo restrictor

A turbo restrictor is a mandatory ring with a fixed-size hole fitted to a turbocharger's air intake, so that the engine can only draw in a limited amount of air and therefore make limited power. In world rallying the FIA introduced one in 1990, starting at 40 mm and later cut to 38 mm and then 34 mm, to hold cars to around 300 bhp. At Rally Catalunya in 1995, officials found that Toyota's restrictors could move, letting air flow around them. Toyota Team Europe was excluded from the 1995 championship, lost all its points and had its competition licence withheld for 12 months.

Sources: evo · Motor Sport Magazine

VTEC

VTEC is Honda's system for switching an engine between two camshaft profiles, one for low-speed smoothness and one for high-rpm power. It stands for Variable Valve Timing & Lift Electronic Control System. Each pair of valves has three cam lobes; at low revs the two mild outer lobes work the valves, and at around 4,800 to 5,200 rpm oil pressure pushes pins that lock the rocker arms to the larger centre lobe, opening the valves earlier, longer and wider. It debuted in the Integra in April 1989, whose 1.6-litre B16A made 160 PS at 7,600 rpm: 100 PS per litre without a turbo.

Sources: Honda Global

Wankel rotary engine

A Wankel rotary engine is an internal-combustion engine with no pistons: instead, a rounded triangular rotor spins on an eccentric shaft inside an oval-like housing, and the spaces between the rotor and the housing take in, compress, burn and exhaust the fuel mixture. Devised by German engineer Felix Wankel, it is compact, light, smooth and happy to rev, but less fuel-efficient than a piston engine and needs oil injected to lubricate its apex seals. Mazda signed a technical cooperation agreement with NSU and Wankel in July 1961 and launched its first rotary car, the Cosmo Sport (110S), in May 1967.
